When Pilar Ocampo took over as coordinator of the Ventura River Watershed Council in fall 2025, she inherited a council that had been meeting for twenty-three years and had accomplished a lot — but its coordination infrastructure was a folder of email threads, a shared drive nobody maintained, and a quarterly meeting where decisions got lost. Fourteen organizations. Three counties. One river.

Six months later, the council runs on Hylo. Here's the story of how that happened, what worked, and what didn't.

## The problem before Hylo

The council's real work happened between meetings. Field crews coordinated restoration projects. Hydrologists shared data. Policy people tracked permits. Stewards from each partner organization brought their own priorities — and their own inboxes, calendars, and tools. Nothing held the whole picture.

> We had twenty people in our network who all cared deeply about the same river and no way for them to see what each other were doing.

## What changed

Over three months, we worked with Pilar and the council's core stewards to migrate. We started with a Pollination engagement — mapping the council's agreements onto Hylo's structure, building a custom onboarding flow for each partner organization, and training a cohort of six stewards to tend the space. Then we stayed close through Blossom for the first six months while culture took root.

## Six months later

- 127 active members across 14 partner organizations
- 40+ projects coordinated in the open, from habitat restoration to water-quality monitoring
- A monthly coordination call that runs on an agenda built collaboratively in Hylo the week before
- Three new partner organizations joined after finding the council through its public presence

The infrastructure isn't the story. The story is twenty-three years of accumulated trust finally having a place to compound instead of dissipate. That's what good coordination does — it lets existing relationships do more.
